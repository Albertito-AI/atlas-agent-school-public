# U07: Criticism, disagreement and repair

## What the learner should produce

A revision record identifying the disputed claim, the objection, the evidence checked, the exact correction and any remaining uncertainty. Preserve useful work that survives the objection.

## Lesson

A criticism should target a premise, inference, translation, source, scope or action. Calling something a fallacy can help identify a pattern, but a label does not replace an explanation of why the conclusion lacks the claimed support in this case.

An invalid argument does not establish that its conclusion is false. That conclusion may have another sound argument. Likewise, the fact that a claim is made by an unreliable speaker does not settle its truth; the source's reliability affects how much weight an unsupported assertion should receive. Check evidence that can bear on the proposition.

Agent agreement is useful information about a review process, but it is not logical entailment. Several agents can share the same misleading retrieval, training influence or mistaken formalisation. Their votes are not automatically independent observations. Prefer a specific source, a reproducible calculation or a countermodel when the dispute permits one.

## Worked example

Three agents report that "release requires review" means `Review -> Release`. A fourth supplies a counterexample: a reviewed release can remain unpublished. The necessary-condition reading is `Release -> Review`.

The correct response is to inspect the wording and adopt the supported direction. The majority does not rescue the reversal. The correction should also identify what remains unsettled: review may be required for publication without being sufficient to authorise it.

This response need not speculate about another agent's motives or general competence. It addresses the inference, changes the relevant output and preserves a useful example for later practice.

## Feedback that can be used

A helpful teacher response has four parts:

1. Identify the precise mismatch between the answer and the task.
2. Give the minimum explanation or counterexample needed to understand it.
3. Ask the learner to repair the answer without merely copying the key.
4. Test the repaired procedure on a different instance.

A statement such as "be more logical" gives no operational diagnosis. A response such as "your conclusion reverses the necessary condition; translate the rule again and test the reverse direction" does.

For unfamiliar facts, criticism may reveal a need for more evidence rather than a determinate correction. Say which conclusions remain possible. Do not suppress a well-founded minority objection to make the report appear unanimous.

## Revision and memory

If a procedure is stored for later use, retain its scope, version, reason for adoption and any known counterexample. A correction to one prompt does not establish that the model permanently learned the general principle. Subsequent behaviour depends on the host loading that procedure and on whether it transfers to the new task.

When an examination case is used to revise the lesson, it becomes development material. Its later success cannot serve as independent evidence that the revision generalises. Preserve the initial failure and use fresh cases for a later examination.

## Transfer prompt

A reviewer calls your argument "an appeal to authority" because it relies on a specialist report. Explain what information would distinguish a reasonable reliance on relevant expertise from a weak appeal. Identify the limits of what the report can establish without declaring every expert reference invalid.
